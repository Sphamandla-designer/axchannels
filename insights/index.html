Insights

Thinking in systems.

Notes from the studio on complexity, clarity and the design decisions in between.

Essay 01

Complexity is a design problem, not a technology problem.

When a digital experience feels complicated, the instinct is to blame the technology — the legacy system, the integrations, the stack. But most complexity that users feel was never technical. It is structural: information organised around the org chart instead of the task, workflows that accumulated exceptions, screens that show everything because nobody decided what matters.

Technology moves complexity around. Design is what removes it.

That is why we start with the system, not the stack. Once the relationships between people, process and data are made explicit, the technical decisions become smaller — and the experience becomes clear almost as a side effect.

Essay 02

Systems before screens.

The interface is the most visible part of a product and the least important place to start. Screens designed before the system are decoration: they inherit every ambiguity underneath them, and no amount of visual polish repays that debt.

A screen can only be as clear as the system it stands on.

We design in order: the relationships, then the structure, then the journey, then — last — the interface. It is slower for the first week and faster for every week after.

Essay 03

Where AI belongs in a business system.

Intelligence is a material, not a strategy. It belongs where decisions are frequent, data-rich and reversible — surfacing the pattern a person would find if they had the time. It does not belong where judgement, accountability or trust are the point.

AI should sharpen the decision, not take it.

In practice that means designing explicit human decision points: the system recommends, shows its reasoning, and waits. Products built this way earn trust; products that skip the human lose it at the first visible mistake.

Essay 04

Evidence over opinion.

Design reviews fill with opinions because opinions are free. Evidence — what users actually did, where the process actually broke, what the data actually shows — is more expensive to gather and much cheaper to build on.

Research becomes insight. Insight becomes decisions. Decisions stay traceable.

Every significant design decision in our work can answer one question: what did we see that made this the right call? When the answer is “nothing yet,” that is not a design decision — it is a hypothesis, and it goes into validation, not into production.
