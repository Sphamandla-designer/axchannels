Products Rarely Become Hard To Use All At Once

They Get There One Reasonable Decision At A Time, Until Nobody Can Find Anything.

  • Features arrive faster than structure

    Each release is sensible on its own. Nothing goes back and decides where the new thing belongs, so the navigation grows sideways.

  • The interface mirrors the database

    Screens are organised around how the system stores things rather than around the task someone opened the product to finish.

  • Journeys are broken across screens

    Every screen works. Getting from the first to the last one takes more steps, tabs and guesses than the job actually requires.

  • Onboarding explains the product, not the first win

    New users are toured through the features instead of walked to the first thing that makes the product worth returning to.

  • Screens drift apart

    Two buttons, three date pickers, four empty states. Without a system, each new screen is designed from memory and the product stops feeling like one thing.

  • The hard screens were never designed

    Errors, empty states, loading, permissions, long tables. They are where people spend real time, and they are usually whatever the framework does by default.

So we work the other way around. The product goal and the journeys decide the structure; the structure decides the screens; the system keeps them consistent as the product grows. More on how the studio works.

What We Design

Six Kinds Of Product Work, Grouped By The Problem They Are Solving Rather Than The Technology Underneath.

01

SaaS Products

Subscription and B2B software designed around the workflow it supports: onboarding that reaches a first result, a workspace that holds up under daily use, and settings that do not become a dumping ground.

02

Mobile Applications

Customer-facing and internal apps for iOS and Android, designed to each platform's conventions — thumb reach, native patterns, offline and interrupted states — rather than a desktop layout made narrow.

03

Web Applications

Browser-based products where people log in and do work. Task flows, state, and the screens between the screens, which is where most web apps quietly become difficult.

04

Dashboards & Admin Systems

Data-heavy and operational interfaces. Mostly a hierarchy problem: deciding what earns the first screen, what is secondary, and what belongs one level down, then designing the tables and filters that carry it.

05

Customer Portals

Where your customers check a balance, track a job, submit something or find a document. Designed so the common task is obvious and the rare one is still findable.

06

Internal & Enterprise Platforms

Products with several roles, permissions and journeys running through the same screens. The work is making one interface serve all of them without becoming everyone's second-best option.

A Product Is Not A Set Of Screens. It Is A Chain Of Decisions

Each Stage Is Decided By The One Above It. That Is Why A Screen-First Redesign Leaves The Original Problem Exactly Where It Was.

  1. 01

    Product Strategy

    What the product is for, who it is for, and which outcome it is measured by.

  2. 02

    User Flows

    The routes people take to finish a task, including the ones that fail partway.

  3. 03

    Information Architecture

    What lives where, what it is called, and how the levels relate to each other.

  4. 04

    Wireframes

    Structure and hierarchy per screen, settled while changing it is still cheap.

  5. 05

    Interface Design

    The visual system: type, colour, density, and the components screens are built from.

  6. 06

    Prototype

    The important journeys made clickable, so they can be judged before they are built.

  7. 07

    Design System

    Components and their states, documented, so the next screen matches the last one.

  8. 08

    Developer Handoff

    Specifications, behaviour and responsive rules, written for the people building it.

  9. 09

    Iteration

    What the product teaches you once it is in use, fed back into the system.

Not every engagement runs all nine. A small MVP may compress strategy, flows and wireframes into a fortnight; an established product may only need the system and the handoff. What does not change is the order: we do not draw screens before the structure they sit in has been agreed.

Eight Stages, Scoped To The Product

Every Stage Ends In Something You Can Look At And Respond To, So Direction Is Agreed While It Is Still Cheap To Change.

  1. 01

    Discover

    Business objectives, the people who use the product, the context it runs in, what is already going wrong, the technical constraints you are working inside, and the workflows that matter most.

    You get a written brief, scope and timeline.

  2. 02

    Define

    Product priorities, the journeys that carry the most value, how features relate to one another, and — where the engagement is an MVP — what is genuinely in the first release and what is not.

    You get priorities, journeys and an agreed scope line.

  3. 03

    Structure

    User flows and task flows, information architecture, navigation, and wireframes. The product is decided here, in grey boxes, before anyone argues about a colour.

    You get flows, an IA map and wireframes.

  4. 04

    Design

    Visual direction, then high-fidelity screens: responsive layouts, interaction patterns, and the reusable components the rest of the product will be assembled from.

    You get high-fidelity screens and a component set.

  5. 05

    Prototype

    The journeys that matter, made clickable and walked through end to end. It is far cheaper to find out that a flow does not work in a prototype than in a sprint review.

    You get an interactive prototype of the key journeys.

  6. 06

    Systemise

    A design system, new or extending yours: components and their states, typography, spacing, colour usage, interaction patterns and responsive behaviour, documented rather than implied.

    You get a documented component library.

  7. 07

    Validate

    Where scope allows: usability testing on the prototype, structured stakeholder review, and iteration on what comes back. Where it does not, we say so upfront rather than implying testing happened.

    You get findings and the changes made from them.

  8. 08

    Handoff

    Specified components, interaction behaviour, responsive rules and design documentation, prepared for the team building it — and we stay reachable through the build rather than going quiet at delivery.

    You get developer-ready files and documentation.

UX And UI Are Not The Same Job

One Decides How It Works. The Other Decides How It Speaks

UX answers how the product should work: what happens, in what order, and what it is called. UI answers how it communicates and behaves: what you notice first, what a control looks like when it is disabled, what the screen does while it waits. A product needs both held to the same goal, which is the part that usually goes missing.

How we approach UX as a studio

  • Can someone finish the task

    The measure that matters. Not whether the screen is admired, but whether the job gets done without a support ticket.

  • Is the next step obvious

    At every point in a flow, one thing should clearly be the next thing. Two equal options is a decision you have handed to the user.

  • Does it behave the same way twice

    The same control doing the same thing everywhere is what lets someone stop reading the interface and start using it.

  • Is the hierarchy honest

    What is emphasised should be what matters, not what the loudest stakeholder asked for.

  • What happens when it goes wrong

    An error that explains what happened and what to do next is interface design. One that says "something went wrong" is not.

  • Does it survive the real data

    Designs made with tidy sample content fall over on a 400-row table and a name that is 60 characters long.

Designing these is most of the difference between a product that demos well and one that is pleasant on a Tuesday afternoon.

Where The Product Uses AI

An AI Feature Is A Trust Problem Before It Is An Interface One

A model that is right most of the time still needs an interface that tells the truth about the rest. Where a product includes AI — a suggestion, a summary, an automated step — the design has to say what it did, how sure it is, and how to disagree with it. We design those patterns. We do not build or train models, and we do not make claims about what a model will do.

  • Say what it did

    What the system acted on and why, in language that does not require knowing how it works.

  • Show the confidence honestly

    An output presented as certain when it is not is the fastest way to lose a user for good.

  • Always leave a way to disagree

    Every generated result needs a visible way to edit, reject or correct it. Being able to correct it is what makes people willing to use it.

  • Design the handoff to a human

    Where the product is unsure, escalating beats guessing — and the route to a person has to be designed, not improvised.

  • Keep the person in control

    Suggest, then let someone accept, edit or ignore. Automatic actions need to be visible, reversible, and turned off somewhere obvious.

  • Design the waiting and the failure

    Generation takes time and sometimes returns nothing useful. Both are states, and both need designing.

Product Design And Website Design Are Two Different Jobs

They Share A Toolkit And Almost Nothing Else. Picking The Wrong One Is The Most Expensive Decision On This Page.

Digital Product Design

  • Someone logs in and completes a task
  • Used repeatedly, often daily
  • Success is the job getting done
  • Built on workflows, state and data
  • Roles, permissions and accounts
  • Grows feature by feature, so it needs a system
  • Measured by adoption and task completion

Website Design

  • Someone arrives, reads, and decides
  • Usually visited once or twice
  • Success is an enquiry or a sale
  • Built on content, structure and search
  • Mostly one anonymous visitor type
  • Grows page by page, so it needs a sitemap
  • Measured by traffic and conversion

If people are reading about your business and deciding whether to get in touch, you want website design and development. If they are logging in to get something done, you want this page. Some businesses need both, and they are scoped and priced separately — a marketing site is not a product, and a product is not a bigger website.

A redesign that repaints every screen and leaves the structure alone moves the problem rather than solving it. We audit what exists, work out which parts are worth keeping, and restructure the part that is actually costing you — usually a handful of journeys rather than the whole product.

  • Screens no longer look like the same product

What is digital product design?

It is the design of software people use repeatedly to get something done: a SaaS platform, an app, a dashboard, a portal. Unlike a website, which is mostly read once, a product is returned to — so the work is about tasks, flows, states and consistency rather than pages and content.

What is the difference between UX/UI design and product design?

UX and UI are two of the disciplines inside product design. UX decides how the product should work, UI decides how it looks and behaves, and product design is the practice that holds both to a product goal and keeps them coherent across every screen, state and release.

Do you design mobile apps?

Yes, for iOS and Android, including apps that sit alongside a web product. We design to each platform's conventions rather than shrinking a desktop layout, and the handoff covers responsive behaviour and interaction states.

Do you design SaaS products?

Yes. Most SaaS work involves onboarding, a main workspace, settings and permissions, and the screens between them — which is where products usually become inconsistent as they grow. That is the work: making the whole thing behave as one product.

Can you redesign an existing product?

Yes, and it is a common engagement. We audit what exists, find where people get stuck, and restructure that rather than repainting every screen. Some usability problems are structural or technical and a visual redesign will not fix them — where that is the case we say so instead of designing around it.

Can you design dashboards?

Yes. Dashboards are mostly a hierarchy problem: deciding what matters, what is secondary and what belongs one level down, then designing the tables, filters, empty states and loading states that carry it.

Can you work with our existing development team?

Yes, and it works best when developers are in the conversation early, while structure is still being decided. We hand over specified components, interaction behaviour and responsive rules, and stay reachable through the build rather than disappearing at handoff.

Do you create prototypes?

Yes. Key journeys are prototyped as clickable flows so they can be walked through, shown to stakeholders and tested before they are built — the cheapest point at which to find out something does not work.

Do you create design systems?

Yes, either a new one or an extension of what you already have. A system covers components and their states, typography, spacing, colour usage, interaction patterns and responsive behaviour, so the product stays consistent as features are added.

Can you design an MVP?

Yes, and most of that work is subtraction. We help decide which journeys genuinely belong in a first release, design those properly, and leave the structure open so the next ones can be added without a rebuild.

Do you also develop the product?

Not as part of this service. AX-Channels builds websites, but product engineering is a separate discipline and we do not claim it. On a product engagement we design, prototype and document, and work with your development team or a partner you choose.

How much does digital product design cost?

There is no fixed price, because a three-screen MVP and a multi-role platform are not the same job. Scope is agreed in discovery and priced against it, in writing, before design starts. Websites are packaged and priced publicly; products are not.

How long does product design take?

It depends on the number of journeys, how settled the requirements are, and how quickly decisions come back. A timeline goes into your proposal once discovery has shown what the work involves. What we commit to upfront is a reply to any enquiry within two working days.
